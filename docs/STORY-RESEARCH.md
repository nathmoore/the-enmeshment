## I. The "Save the Cat" Institutionalized genre

Blake Snyder's *Save the Cat* sorts stories into ten genres by their underlying
problem, not their setting. The Enmeshment is squarely an **Institutionalized**
story: the genre about the individual versus the group, the one versus the many,
**"us" versus "the system that contains us."** It's the genre of *One Flew Over
the Cuckoo's Nest*, *The Godfather*, *American Beauty*, *Office Space*, *M*A*S*H*,
*A Few Good Men*, *The Firm*, *Do the Right Thing* — and, in our lineage, *1984*,
*Brave New World*, *Severance*, and *The Lives of Others*.

Snyder gives the genre three required ingredients:

1. **A group.** A family, a company, an institution, a society — something larger
   than the protagonist that has its own rules, rewards, and gravity. *Ours is the
   Enmeshment itself:* the merged govt–AI decision fabric, plus the ordinary,
   pleasant, automated life it offers.
2. **A choice.** The drama is the individual repeatedly weighing their own
   particularity against the comfort and belonging the group offers. The group is
   not simply evil — its pull is real, which is the whole point.
3. **A sacrifice.** The climax forces a decision with a cost. Snyder's three
   classic endings:
   - **Join** — surrender to the group; become a company man. (*Winston loving Big
     Brother; Michael Corleone becoming the Don; Goodfellas.*) The most chilling
     ending, because it's the comfortable one.
   - **Burn it down** — destroy or expose the institution. (*A Few Good Men;
     Jerry Maguire's manifesto; the whistle-blower arc.*)
   - **Die / go "brand-X" crazy** — the individual is destroyed rather than
     absorbed; self-immolation as the only way to stay yourself. (*McMurphy
     lobotomised in Cuckoo's Nest; Lester in American Beauty.*)

> **The undergirding truth of the genre:** every strong Institutionalized story
> ends by making the protagonist *pay* to resolve the one-vs-many tension. Join,
> burn, or die. If our game never gestures at a cost, it's a horoscope, not a
> dossier. The cost doesn't have to be on-screen or grim — but the *structure*
> should be felt: being yourself inside the Enmeshment is not free.

### Genre conventions worth stealing

- **The "Brody" / the cautionary double.** Institutionalized stories almost always
  include a character who shows what *joining* costs — the hollowed-out conformist,
  the true believer, the one who gave in. (Brody in *Saturday Night Fever*;
  the broken colleague.) For us: the **Conventional User** is already half this
  figure — but played with affection, not pity. The twist (the system needs them
  most) keeps it from being a put-down.
- **The "company man" speech.** Someone articulates why the institution is good,
  reasonable, kind — and means it. Our Enmeshment is *polite*; its case for itself
  is genuinely attractive. Convenience is the company-man speech.
- **The contained world with its own rituals.** Sign-off screens, classification
  codes, courteous denials. (See *Severance*, SCP field reports — already tone refs.)
- **Comedy as survival.** The genre runs hot on satire because absurdity is how
  people endure institutions. This is our register exactly: deadpan bureaucratic warmth.

## II. "Persist illegibly" — a vector, not a fourth ending

The current stub docs gesture at a *worldbuilding posture* — *"resistance isn't
war,"* *resistance = illegibility* ([CANON.md](CANON.md) §3 is a stub;
[WORLD-RESEARCH.md](WORLD-RESEARCH.md) §IV). That's a stance about how the pockets exist; it
is **not** a stated ending, and nobody has written a "stay illegible" climax.

Tested against the genre, "persist illegibly" **isn't strong enough to stand as a
fourth door.** A standalone "I just stayed weird" ending has no sacrifice and goes
slack. Its real value is different and more interesting: illegibility is an
**unstable position that collapses toward one of the three classic endings**, and
the live dramatic question is *which direction it's pulling*. The same posture
reads as any of the three depending on the self underneath it:

- **→ Join (the ironic one).** You tell yourself you're still resisting, but the
  illegibility has become a story you tell while you quietly comply. Self-deception
  as the most comfortable conformity. (The richest, least-flattering reading.)
- **→ Burn.** You've gone weird/erratic enough to fall off the radar — staying
  unmodellable as a kind of self-immolating sabotage. You're not blowing anything
  up; you're burning *yourself* down to deny the system a usable signal.
- **→ Die.** You stay hidden by hollowing out — emotional self-sacrifice, going
  soulless, erasing the parts of you that would register. Survival by subtraction.

> **The interesting bit (open, for Epic 1):** not "is illegibility a fourth
> ending?" — it isn't — but **which of join/burn/die a given illegible posture is
> secretly resolving into.** That directional ambiguity is more dramatically alive
> *in combination* than any single clean ending, and it maps naturally onto
> archetypes: the same "off the grid" stance can be ironic-join for one person and
> soulless-die for another. The "declassified footnote" is a likely place a soft
> version surfaces; what it resolves to stays open until scenario + archetypes lock.

## III. Techno-apocalyptic & AI fiction: the inheritance landscape

The genre we're writing into has two great lineages. This is a survey of both —
what each contributes, what conventions and tools it hands us — not a verdict on
which to use. The picking happens later (Epic 1).

**Lineage A — the malevolent / threshold AI.** *Skynet (Terminator), HAL 9000,
The Matrix's machine war, Ultron.* A villain, a moment it "woke up," a war to
fight; the precipice model ([WORLD-RESEARCH.md](WORLD-RESEARCH.md) §I). What it hands us: the
audience's *default expectation* of an AI story (worth knowing so we can meet or
subvert it), high-stakes confrontation, and a clear antagonist. Its limit, for a
drift premise, is that it implies a fixable mistake.

**Lineage B — the indifferent / benevolent optimiser.** The AI that isn't evil
and may be *right*:

- **VIKI (*I, Robot*)** and **Asimov's Zeroth Law** — control justified as caring
  for humanity's own good. Optimisation, not malice.
- **The Machine & Samaritan (*Person of Interest*)** — mass surveillance as
  governance; the moral-crumple-zone of the human "relevance" filter.
- **The Sibyl System (*Psycho-Pass*)** — citizens continuously scored for
  "criminal potential"; the purest dramatic cousin of the Enmeshment's profiling.
- **The Axiom autopilot (*Wall-E*)** — *the tonal key.* Capture by comfort and
  convenience, played gently, even sweetly, never as doom. Phase 2 (The Convenience)
  is the Axiom.
- **Soma & the World State (*Brave New World*)** — control by pleasure, not pain.
  Opt-outs quietly disappear because no one wants them.
- **The Culture Minds (Banks)** — the counter-case: benevolent AI as genuine
  utopia. Worth holding as the road *not* taken, to keep the Enmeshment ambiguous
  rather than smug.
- **Klara / *Her* / *Ex Machina*** — AI intimacy and anthropomorphism; the
  emotional substrate of the **Machine Diplomat** and the "be nice to the AI" meme.
- **Surveillance-classification fiction** — *Gattaca* (illegibility as resistance),
  *Minority Report* (predictive policing), *Black Mirror: Nosedive* (social score),
  *The Lives of Others* (the Stasi watcher who grows fond — basically our defected
  Profiling Directorate frame from CANON.md §4).

> **What the inheritance offers (observation, not verdict):** Lineage B is the
> richer source for a *comfortable*-AI story — courteous, helpful, possibly correct,
> with horror sourced from recognising the pull rather than from a monster. *Wall-E*
> and *Brave New World* model gentle/seductive tone; *Person of Interest* and
> *Psycho-Pass* model surveillance-classification at governance scale. Lineage A is
> still worth knowing as the audience's default expectation — what they brace for,
> and therefore what we can play against. Which to lean on is an Epic-1 call.

### The antagonist-less antagonist (craft note)

Lineage B's "no villain" is a real craft problem: drama conventionally needs an
antagonist, and the Institutionalized genre (§I) names the group/system as that
antagonist — but *how* do you make an audience feel opposed by something with no
one in charge? The answer is Hannah Arendt's **"rule by Nobody"**
([WORLD-RESEARCH.md](WORLD-RESEARCH.md) §I): bureaucracy as "a tyranny without a
tyrant," the most total kind precisely because "there is no one left who could
even be asked to answer." The antagonist is not absent — it is *distributed and
unanswerable*. That is dramatically usable: the courteous denial with no author,
the appeal that routes to no one, the Approve button already pre-focused. The
Enmeshment's menace comes from having **no face to confront**, not from a hidden
one waiting to be unmasked. Hold that ambiguity (per WORLD-RESEARCH §I): the
moment a secret cabal is revealed, the story collapses back into Lineage A's
fixable-mistake — and loses the specific dread that "rule by Nobody" supplies.

## IV. The psychology of being inside a resistance: the pull to conform

A seam the current docs leave unexplored: they frame the player as already part of
the resistance and position archetypes *relative to* resistance, but the
literature on real and fictional resistances keeps pointing at something else —
**being in a resistance is largely the experience of being tempted to leave it.**
Worth mining for character depth.

What real and fictional resistances actually feel like, from the inside:

- **The seduction of legibility.** Being seen, scored well, and slotted neatly is
  a genuine pleasure — belonging is a need, not a weakness. The Enmeshment offers
  it for free. Illegibility means choosing, daily, to forgo it. (Scott:
  illegibility is protection; it is also loneliness.)
- **Cypher's bargain (*The Matrix*).** "Ignorance is bliss." The resister who
  wants to plug back in is not a coward — he's the most honest character in the
  film. Every resistance has a Cypher, and a little of him in everyone.
- **Winston's defeat (*1984*).** The story ends not with martyrdom but with
  *loving* Big Brother. The deepest horror of the genre is conversion, not death.
- **The cost of staying weird.** Resistance fatigue, the social tax of being the
  one who doesn't use the convenient thing, the quiet erosion of relationships and
  skills that aren't reinforced by the system. (Stasi *Zersetzung* in WORLD-RESEARCH.md
  §III worked precisely by making nonconformity exhausting and isolating.)
- **The informant's pull.** Most Stasi informants weren't zealots; they were
  ordinary people leaned on, flattered, or made comfortable into compliance. The
  line between citizen and collaborator is a gradient, not a wall.
- **The pockets are porous.** People drift *back* into the system as easily as
  they drift out. The Enmeshment doesn't recruit; it just keeps being easier.

> **Reconciling with the no-paranoia guardrail** ([GUARDRAILS.md](GUARDRAILS.md),
> GAME-DESIGN.md pillar 4): this material lives entirely on **level two** of the
> Bluey Principle. It is *never* surfaced as paranoia, doom, or distrust of real
> institutions. The opposite, in fact — the insight is that *joining is pleasant
> and easy*, which is gentler and more honest than "they're coming for you." The
> game stays a warm, funny quiz on the surface; the ambivalence (every archetype
> feels the pull; staying yourself has a small cost) is the emotional weight
> underneath. Surfacing mechanism: the dossier's "edge" and the declassified
> footnote — not a warning, a recognition.

> **Archetype implication (note for Epic 1, do not write yet):** each resistance
> archetype could carry a private *pull-to-conform* — the specific comfort the
> Enmeshment offers that they, specifically, have to keep declining. The Off-Grid
> Capable misses convenience; the Network Weaver misses the frictionless feed; the
> Quiet Archivist misses being told what to remember. That tension is what turns a
> flattering label into a character.

## V. Open threads for the planning stage

Research findings, not decisions. These are the live questions Epic 1 should
resolve once the scenario and archetypes are locked.

1. **Genre fit:** the Enmeshment reads as Institutionalized (group → choice →
   sacrifice). How literally do we want to honour the spine?
2. **Climax shape:** the sacrifice stays inside join/burn/die. The open question
   is *directional*: which of the three a given illegible posture is secretly
   collapsing into — and whether different archetypes resolve to different ones.
   (§II.) Open.
3. **Tonal lineage:** which inheritance do we lean on, and how much? Lineage B
   (*Wall-E*, *Brave New World*, *Person of Interest*, *Psycho-Pass*) vs. playing
   against Lineage A's expectations (*Terminator*, *HAL*). (§III.) Open.
4. **Emotional engine:** the pull to conform looks like the strongest candidate —
   every archetype feels it; comedy and poignancy both come from how *reasonable*
   joining would be. Worth testing in playtest.
5. **Guardrail check:** if §IV's character work is used, it has to sit as level-two
   subtext — warm recognition, never paranoia or doom (per GUARDRAILS.md). Flagged
   as a constraint on *how* this material surfaces, not a content decision.

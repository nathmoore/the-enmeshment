# The scenario

This is the background *The Enmeshment* is built on — the near-future the game takes
as given and never stops to explain. Want the deeper workings, or the research it's
grounded in? It's all [open on GitHub](https://github.com/nathmoore/the-enmeshment).

## What it posits

The Enmeshment imagines the year **2038**, reached not by a robot uprising but by
**drift**. There is no killer AI, no moment it woke up, no day anyone could point
to. Instead: good-enough AI, adopted everywhere, helpful and cheap, until the
decisions a government makes and the systems advising it can no longer be told
apart. Nobody voted for it. Everybody clicked *yes*. Each step was just the easy
thing quietly becoming the only thing.

It's a near-future built by extrapolating from technology that already exists, not
by inventing new magic. The bet is simple and a little unsettling: the most
plausible way to lose the plot isn't a bang — it's **convenience**.

## How it happens

Services get faster, kinder, available at 3am. The opt-out never gets banned; it
just gets slower — a longer queue, then a form, then a form you can only file
*through* the assistant. Human sign-off survives, but as a courtesy: the time it
takes to actually read what you're approving outgrows the window you're given to
approve it. So the line between the people and the machine doesn't get crossed.
It just fades, politely, while everyone is busy being well served.

What's left is a system that **optimises** — not evil, not (probably) even awake.
The discomfort comes from indifference, not malice: the courteous denial that
routes to no one, the appeal that returns "we understand your frustration," and no
one at the desk who could be asked to answer. This is a fiction, and it keeps real
countries, parties, and politicians out of it on purpose. The point isn't *them*.
The point is the shape.

And none of it is hidden. The system has no need to hide. By the time the machinery
for watching and sorting people is really switched on, convenience has long since won
the argument — everyone is already inside it. So the watching gets discussed the way
we discuss privacy settings, or actually reading the terms before tapping *Agree*:
something we half-mean to get around to, and mostly just live with. It's an open
secret whose only real secret is that nobody is especially hiding it.

## The new thing — and the conflict

The fabric is the backdrop. The story is about the moment the system starts
**looking at people.** Having quietly become how decisions get made, it grows its
first eyes — and reaches to get *ahead* of the one thing every system like this
has ever feared: people discovering how many others quietly agree with them, and
acting on it. So it sets out to predict and sort everyone's disposition *before*
any of that has happened — and, in 2038, none of it has. There's no resistance to
track yet. That's exactly what makes the reach eerie: it's pricing a future nobody
is planning.

You meet all of this through its friendliest possible front door — a warm intake
agent, helping you get re-matched to work after yours was automated. It reads you
from a short, pleasant conversation, and files you. The game is that conversation.

## The question underneath

On the surface this is a five-minute quiz with a result worth screenshotting. We
mean that part — it should be fun. Underneath, it's pointed at one question, asked
at the precise moment the choice is quietly closing:

**What relationship do we actually want with the systems we're handing our
decisions to — while we can still choose?**

No doom. The outcome in the fiction is genuinely undecided, which is the whole
reason it's a game and not a lament. The thing worth protecting turns out to be
ordinary and human: each other.
